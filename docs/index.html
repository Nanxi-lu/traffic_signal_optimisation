Traffic Signal Optimisation

How fixed-time signal coordination, green allocation and offsets change delay on a synthetic three-junction corridor, evaluated with vehicle-level queue simulation, matched experiments and movement fairness checks.

Code on GitHub Full methodology Related: Liverpool bus panel

Synthetic and experimental. All traffic demand is generated, the model is not field calibrated, and no timing plan has been deployed. Every number on this page is read from the saved Phase 4G results; nothing here runs a simulation.

Main finding

PM peak, Integrated strategy compared with the original coordinated baseline (Phase 3A), across 30 matched arrival seeds.

Network-wide delay reductions do not necessarily imply improvements for every traffic movement. The integrated PM timing strategy reduced simulated network delay but exceeded the predefined eastbound fairness threshold against the original baseline. Against the fairness-protected Phase 4E plan, the same candidate passes.

The synthetic corridor

Three signalised junctions on a hypothetical road. Each junction runs two fixed-time phases: east-west green (EB and WB together), then north-south green.

EB and WB travel the full corridor at a constant 40 km/h. North-south vehicles are one-junction local trips. Queues are first-in, first-out with unlimited storage and a 2 s discharge headway.

Protected movements (EB, WB, C-N, C-S) pass when the upper end of their 95% interval is no more than +2 s per vehicle. This is an experimental threshold, not an engineering or legal standard, and each interval is individual rather than a simultaneous guarantee. Side-road movements at A and B were never given a criterion.

How it works

Synthetic demand
A seeded day of 15-minute counts with AM and PM peaks, split into four operating periods.
Webster timing
Critical flow ratios against an assumed 1,800 veh/h saturation flow give a cycle from C = (1.5L + 5) / (1 − Y), with 12 s lost time, bounded to 40 to 150 s.
Coordination and offsets
A common cycle per period, then offsets searched for progression and simulated delay under a directional fairness constraint, using separate training and holdout seeds.
Green allocation
Junction C's split and offset were searched jointly in the peaks (Phase 4F-3B).
Paired audit
Phase 4G froze three strategies and ran each on the same 30 seeds (500 to 529) in four representative hours, pairing results by seed and movement.

Limitations

  • Synthetic rather than observed traffic counts.
  • Unlimited queue storage, so no spillback or junction blocking.
  • Simplified two-phase control with no actuation or plan transitions.
  • Constant speed and fixed discharge headway.
  • No turning movements, lane groups or pedestrian phases.
  • No field calibration or validation.
  • Four representative hours, not a continuous day.
  • Local searches only; no plan is claimed to be optimal.

Related project

The Liverpool bus real-time panel uses GTFS schedules and real-time vehicle location data to measure how Route 10A journeys compare with their timetabled runtimes. It shows when journeys ran late, but its data cannot separate junction delay from other causes. This project looks at one of those causes from the other direction: it simulates how signal timing distributes delay, on a synthetic corridor. It makes no claim about bus performance in Liverpool.
